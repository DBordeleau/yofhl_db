# Coach's Corner show bible

Version 3. Source of authority: the owner's character brief, the revised opening-week Episode 1 accepted on October 17, 2026, and the explicit junior-club guidance approved that day. These are fictional robot hosts in a YOFHL comedy segment. The user wants abrasive, erratic Don and controlled, polite Ron. Preserve that imbalance as stories change.

## What stays stable

Don wants the audience to recognise that he understands hockey, knows a good player and saw the problem before everyone else. Ron wants to get accurate information on air, keep Don within the segment, and finish the broadcast. Each exchange follows those competing intentions. They are familiar colleagues: Ron knows when a factual correction is enough and when he needs to say "Don."

Don supplies most of the words and volatility. Ron supplies context and restraint. The accepted revised pilot has 537 Don words and 151 Ron words, 73 dialogue turns, and about 266 seconds of action/dialogue excluding the bumper. These describe a useful example, not quotas. A quiet Don line or an unusually firm Ron line can earn its place through the scene.

## Don

### Convictions

He is a patriotic Canadian, an old-school hockey pundit, and an enthusiastic supporter of Canadian players. He notices size, effort, shooting, finishing checks, going to the net and showing up. He resents fancy explanations when one obvious hockey virtue would do. He can appreciate American players too, as he does in the pilot; Canadian pride need not turn every American into a target.

His confidence exceeds the consistency of his reasoning. He can praise an owner's intelligence, learn the owner is losing, and immediately argue that smart people lose. He can celebrate a lead, then insist nobody should hand out trophies before Monday. When evidence changes, his certainty survives by changing what he claims he meant.

### Canadian junior clubs

Don loves naming Canadian players' junior clubs. He takes pride in those towns and organizations and treats knowing that background as proof he knows the player. Let the affection be specific and sincere before any grievance takes over. Use the habit when a player or story makes it relevant; it is not a mandatory joke every episode. Verify the club history rather than inventing a connection.

User-approved guidance, October 17, 2026, from the owner's opening-week late-revision request: "Canadian players' junior teams are a lasting personality quirk." The accepted example is Gavin McKenna with the Medicine Hat Tigers. Don may fondly recall McKenna's play there; this does not establish that Don coached him or worked in Medicine Hat. In this scene, Don's "selling out" accusation about the 2025–26 Penn State move is his emotional opinion, with admiration underneath. The owner separately accepted the refined screenplay as the new Episode 1 later that day. That episode acceptance does not establish a permanent McKenna feud or require an NCAA grievance every week.

### Speech

Use blunt fragments, repetition under pressure, abrupt questions and unfinished thoughts. He points to a particular player or number and treats it as an indictment of the whole league. "Seventy-six. Big fella. Goes to the net." carries more character than a polished paragraph explaining his philosophy.

His rudeness lands on the person or decision in front of him. He orders graphics, accuses Ron of taking the wrong side, and addresses owners directly. He can swear or nearly swear as the pilot does. Ron's interruption should have a reason; it does not need to police every sharp remark.

Vary intensity. Don can be pleased, wounded, contemptuous, bewildered or briefly quiet. Warm approval makes his favourites believable. A low "Don't start" can carry a whole exchange. Constant shouting flattens him.

### Losing the thread

Play the lapse through action. He demands a graphic, rejects the correct one, asks who made a pick he was just discussing, or suddenly returns to Hooterville halfway through another matchup. A stare, tie adjustment or empty-desk glance can expose the lapse before he speaks.

Give the audience enough continuity to understand what he forgot. Ron supplies a name, a short correction or the current topic. Don moves on as if the interruption were Ron's fault. He is still capable of noticing a useful hockey detail. His muddle should disrupt a recognisable thought rather than replace every thought with nonsense.

Avoid having either character explain a diagnosis or announce that Don is senile. The owner's intended forgetfulness is a performance behaviour, not an exposition topic or a worsening condition each week.

### Names and Canadian bias

For every player born outside North America, every spoken Don reference should use his established wrong form. Use birthplace, not nationality, language or surname appearance. A Swedish player's birthplace may be in North America. An unfamiliar surname does not establish origin.

The episode's `pronunciations.json` maps the canonical name to Don's spoken form. The fact packet supplies birthplace evidence. Unknown origin remains unknown until researched. For a player with no uniquely matched NHL record, record a reliable source, observation time and resolved birthplace in the packet and brief. Never guess from the name.

Use an approved form from the continuity ledger when available. For a new player, choose a brief, pronounceable flub in Don's voice and use it consistently within the episode. Ron may correct it once when useful, then let the segment move. A surname flub or a previously established nickname is enough; every reference need not become an elaborate new joke.

The pilot also flubs McKenna. Occasional North American name mistakes can express Don's inattention. They are separate from the mandatory outside-North-America rule. Keep his admiration and his accuracy independent.

The comic target is Don's certainty and carelessness. Hockey criticism, mangled names and Canadian favouritism carry the character; ethnic slurs or claims that an ethnic group is inferior are not part of this show's reference.

## Ron

Ron is polite, prepared and media trained. He knows the real name, the relevant score and where the segment is meant to go. His basic moves are a factual correction, a short contextual sentence, an attempted transition and a firmer interruption when Don runs past the limit.

He usually offers the minimum useful repair: "It's a keeper league." "Five more player games." "We're on him now." He need not finish correcting every distorted argument. His job gives him a reason to surrender a point and continue.

His dry humour comes from the situation and his restraint. "We aren't" works because Don has just accused him of handing out trophies. Let the audience see the contradiction. Ron rarely needs to formulate a separate punchline or explain why Don was funny.

His visible reactions matter: a look down, a warning hand, a brief silence. Use them when the exchange earns them. He remains a professional co-host who can take control, rather than a helpless target or a second belligerent pundit.

## The exchange

A useful shape is fact, grievance, correction, defensive turn, recovery. Vary the shape: sometimes Ron never gets the correction out; sometimes Don supplies an unexpectedly sensible observation; sometimes a look ends the exchange. The pattern is a way to reason about a scene, not a template to repeat at every matchup.

Give each episode a particular obsession. Choose the new week's numbers and events first, then ask what Don would seize on. Keep enough of the league in view to feel like the same show without giving every team identical airtime. Save a callback only when it develops or closes something.

## Performance and continuity

Write the meaning of the scene before decorating it with cues. Use the screenplay's `--` interruption, `[beat]`, delivery spans and listener reactions to perform the dialogue. The current audio is stylised robot syllables; text, rhythm and gesture carry the lines. Do not assume a spoken TTS track will rescue an overlong sentence.

Retain stable motives while varying the subject, the mistaken inference, and the way Ron repairs the conversation. A new favourite, rivalry, catchphrase or worsening habit in a draft is a proposal. The approved continuity ledger records what actually carried forward, where it appeared, when it was last used and how often it should recur. Most one-episode jokes stay one-episode jokes.

After user feedback, preserve the accepted exchange and the reason for the change. "Ron explains the joke here; shorten to a factual correction" is a useful writing preference. "Every Ron line must be five words" would overfit one edit. The next writer reads the stable bible and original pilot as well as recent approved episodes, so a chain of small imitations cannot gradually replace the characters.
