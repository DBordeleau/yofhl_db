# Annotated opening-week pilot

Read `lib/coachs-corner/pilot.ts` alongside these notes. It is the canonical performed script; `assets/coachs-corner/scripts/opening-week.transcript.txt` is a plain-dialogue companion. These annotations use searchable dialogue anchors so line-number changes do not break them. The pilot's October 15, 2026 scores and schedule remarks belong to that episode.

## Opening: "The other thing. With the kid."

Don is pointing before the monitor is on. Ron tries to introduce the subject, gets interrupted, and has to name it for Don. Bars, static, standings and a dark screen express Don's vague instruction. The graphics are part of the argument. Ron's "The draft" is the smallest useful intervention.

Carry forward the mismatch between Don's certainty and his instructions. Find a fresh trigger; replaying the same wrong-monitor opening every week would turn an observed behaviour into a stock routine.

## Junior-club loyalty: "And the Tigers helped make that name"

This exchange was accepted as part of the revised Episode 1 on October 17, 2026. Don's affection for McKenna is particular: Medicine Hat, the Tigers, his hands, the town. His pride becomes resentment at the 2025–26 Penn State move. "I call that selling out" makes the accusation his personal judgment, not a reported financial fact.

Ron explains NIL through a short devil's-advocate question. Don picks up "name"; Ron's defense of the player's hockey and financial choices gives him "for him" to resent next. The argument follows what each man just said. Don can still say "Hell of a player" warmly before erupting. Ron's silent look lets the dispute end; Don abruptly returns to Boldy.

The owner liked the Medicine Hat warmth but rejected the initial stiff NCAA rant, lecture-like explanation and broad bold/flat cues. Preserve the revised response pattern and changes in intensity, without turning this particular grievance or final shout into a weekly formula. The original version remains in the approved episode's revision archive.

## Status threat: "I KNOW IT'S A KEEPER LEAGUE, RON."

Ron provides relevant context. Don hears a challenge to his competence. He starts a declaration of authority, interrupts himself with a tie adjustment, and asks "Who took him?" He remembers his indignation more reliably than its target.

"We'll get to him" followed by "We're on him now" is the calibration exchange for both hosts. Don has lost the conversational location. Ron supplies it without writing a joke about Don's age. Let the silence and stare do work.

## Affection: "SEVENTY-SIXTH!"

Wilson's draft position becomes an organising grievance. Don's approval is specific and physical: size, going to the net. He contrasts that with mangled names, Ron defends the players, and Don complains that he has been accused of hating everybody. His defensiveness changes the argument before Ron can finish.

The graphic uses correct names and real comparisons. Don's spoken forms are performance, not player IDs. A weekly writer must preserve that separation so misspellings do not break headshots or stat lookup.

## Silence: "Hamhung Hall Monitors."

The 1.9-second stare and slow "Alright" are the reaction. Don does not need a fully explained team-name joke. This is evidence that quiet can be an intentional comic beat. Its duration is specific to this line; another pause needs its own playback judgment.

## Contradiction: "You can be smart and lose. I coached Boston."

Don has just praised an owner and learned that owner is trailing. He protects his earlier assessment by narrowing the claim. His own example creates an implication that he immediately polices: "Don't start." Ron has only looked down.

Carry forward the defensive adjustment and the relationship's familiarity. An unrelated insult about Boston would copy the subject without the mechanism.

## Precision: "Five more player games."

Don notices a real qualification to a points lead. His observation has merit, then his irritation makes it excessive. Ron corrects the unit. Don treats that as evidence Ron refuses to listen, before agreeing it is a close matchup.

Don should still be able to find a useful detail. Ron should still be factually precise. Neither needs to win an extended debate.

## Sudden return: "HOOTERVILLE."

Don is appreciating Kyle Connor, begins to explain, and abruptly returns to an earlier subject. The unfinished clause, sudden point and monitor cut make the thought change visible. The subsequent one-goal/eighteen-assist grievance is tied to concrete league data.

"They're winning" does not settle the matter for Don. It makes him angry at the opponent as well. That escalation grows out of his premise. This is a stronger model for a weekly rant than collecting unrelated complaints.

## Clock and hypocrisy: "Why are we sitting here handing out trophies on Thursday?"

Don has been drawing sweeping conclusions from unfinished matchups, then insists everyone wait until Monday. Ron's "We aren't" is enough. The schedule belongs to the captured scoring period, not a generic seven-day week. Future writers must use the fact packet's real dates.

The hits comparison also shows why editorial review distinguishes a deliberate Don exaggeration from an accidental factual error: 29 versus 17 is not literally twice. Record intentional distortions in the review; keep the monitor and Ron accurate.

## Exit: microphone, warning, Wilson

Don starts removing his microphone early, is told there are twenty seconds left, and chooses that moment to start another address. Ron's firmer "Don" interrupts the near-swear. Don retroactively claims to have said "bad picks." Wilson's return closes the established obsession.

Use a closing callback when it pays off the episode's own material. Wilson is an established favourite, not a mandatory final line.

## Calibration contrasts

These are deliberately weak alternatives, followed by the behaviour the pilot demonstrates:

| Weak alternative | What to preserve instead |
| --- | --- |
| Don explains in a fluent paragraph why advanced statistics undervalue grit. | A concrete number, a physical hockey virtue, a leap in logic, and an interruption. |
| Don inserts a random unrelated word to sound confused. | A traceable lapse that Ron can repair in a few words. |
| Ron delivers an elaborate comeback about Don's intelligence. | A literal correction or restrained reaction that exposes the contradiction. |
| Every Don line is shouted. | Contrast between warmth, muttering, stunned silence, firm criticism and occasional outbursts. |
| Every misspelling becomes a separate correction routine. | Stable wrong forms, selective corrections, and enough momentum to finish the story. |
| Every episode ends with the identical Wilson line. | A callback to the new episode's own grievance, using old favourites when earned. |

Judge a new scene by its intentions and response pattern. The goal is new dialogue that the same two characters would plausibly produce.
